Over-modulation & the envelope's blind spot

Why the envelope detector throws away the sign — EE341, Lecture 2 material

An envelope detector measures a magnitude. Magnitudes have no sign.

Every idea on this page — over-modulation, the π phase shift in your notes, "different x(t)'s give the same envelope", why DSB-SC needs coherent detection — is that one sentence, seen from a different angle.

1. Drag μ and watch it break

The transmitted signal is x_c(t) = A(1 + μ·x(t))·cos(2πf_c t). The quantity A(1+μx) is the signed envelope — what we want the receiver to see. The detector actually outputs A|1+μx|. Below μ = 1 those are the same thing. Above it, they are not.

2. What is actually happening at the fold

Look at the middle panel as you push μ past 1. Three things happen at the same instant, and they are the same event described three ways:

DescriptionWhat you see
Algebra1 + μx(t) passes through zero and goes negative.
WaveformThe carrier shrinks to zero amplitude, then grows back — but inverted. This is your notes' "π phase shift as x(t) < 0".
DetectorThe absolute value folds the negative part back up. The output bounces off zero instead of passing through it.

Why the sign is unrecoverable. A carrier of amplitude −0.5 and a carrier of amplitude +0.5 shifted by 180° are the same waveform. Not similar — identical, sample for sample. The minus sign has been converted into phase. An envelope detector is a diode and an RC: it responds to how big the wiggles are and has no phase reference whatsoever, so that information is gone before the signal ever reaches the output.

A coherent detector does have a phase reference, which is exactly why it can demodulate DSB-SC where an envelope detector cannot.

3. "Different x(t)'s can still give us the same envelope"

This line in your notes is the same fact stated backwards. If the detector only sees |·|, then any two messages with the same magnitude produce the same output, and the receiver has no way to tell them apart.

Here are three genuinely different messages. Look at the top panel — they are clearly not the same signal. Then look at the envelope in the bottom panel.

the three candidate messages resulting DSB-SC envelope A|x(t)| — identical for all three

Switch between them: the top trace changes, the bottom trace does not move at all.

So envelope detection is only valid when the mapping is one-to-one. Adding the carrier — the "+1" in 1 + μx — lifts the whole message onto a positive pedestal so that |1+μx| = 1+μx and no folding can occur. That is what the carrier is for. It buys uniqueness, and you pay for it with the transmit power computed in your efficiency formula.

4. DSB-SC is permanent over-modulation

Remove the carrier: x_c(t) = A·x(t)·cos(2πf_c t). The signed envelope is A·x(t), which goes negative every time the message does. There is no pedestal to prevent it.

So DSB-SC is the μ → ∞ limit of what you are looking at above — folding at every zero crossing of the message. Set the slider to its maximum and you will see the DSB-AM waveform approach exactly the picture on page 3 of your notes, with a phase reversal at each crossing.

envelope detection fails for DSB-SC   ⇔   DSB-SC is always over-modulated

These are not two facts to memorise. They are one fact.

5. The RC envelope detector, and its own two failure modes

Your notes say: "can get envelope by connecting local maximas, or pass thru diode + RC to get peaks." The diode charges the capacitor at each carrier peak; between peaks the capacitor discharges through R. The time constant has to sit in a window:

1/fc  <<  RC  <<  1/W

Drag the time constant and watch both walls:

rectified carrier (diode output) capacitor voltage — the recovered envelope ideal envelope

Too small (RC < 1/fc): the capacitor discharges fully between carrier peaks. The output is just the rectified carrier — massive ripple at fc, no smoothing at all.

Too large (RC > 1/W): the capacitor cannot discharge fast enough to follow the envelope down. The output holds too high and cuts corners on the falling edges — diagonal clipping (also called failure-to-follow distortion). This is the more commonly examined of the two.

6. Checklist

  • Condition: μ|x(t)| ≤ 1 for all t, i.e. 1 + μx ≥ 0. With x normalised to max|x| = 1, this is μ ≤ 1.
  • Only the negative peak binds. Nothing breaks when 1+μx gets large; it breaks when it tries to go below zero. For an asymmetric message the constraint is μ ≤ 1/|min x|.
  • Failure mechanism: the detector outputs a magnitude, so a sign change becomes a 180° phase reversal that the detector cannot see. The folding is irreversible.
  • Also: over-modulation is a nonlinear operation, so it generates harmonics that widen the transmitted bandwidth past 2W and spill into adjacent channels. It is a regulatory problem, not only an audio-quality one.
  • Assumption throughout: x(t) has no DC component — otherwise the DC would be indistinguishable from the carrier.
  • RC detector: 1/f_c << RC << 1/W. Too small → ripple. Too large → diagonal clipping.
  • Trade-off to state: efficiency η = μ²Px/(1+μ²Px) rises with μ, but μ = 1 is a hard wall. Broadcasters run 0.8–0.9 for headroom.
