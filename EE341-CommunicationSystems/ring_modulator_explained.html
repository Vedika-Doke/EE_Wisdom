The Ring Modulator, one step at a time

Why four diodes and two transformers produce DSB-SC — EE341, Communication Systems I

The whole circuit is one thing: a switch that flips the message's polarity fc times per second.

The diodes are not amplifying, mixing, or shaping anything. They are acting as switches, and the carrier is the thing throwing the switch. Once you see that, every formula on your notes page follows in two lines.

1. Start from the answer, then work backwards

Here is what the circuit does, written out. Everything below is just an explanation of where this comes from:

vout(t)  =  x(t) · c(t)    where c(t) = ±1 square wave at frequency fc

So during one half of every carrier cycle the output is the message, and during the other half it is the message upside down. Nothing else happens. The circuit is a polarity flipper.

2. Watch it flip

The animation below shows the same instant in three places at once: which diodes are conducting, how the message reaches the output transformer, and what the output waveform is doing. Let it run, or scrub the slider.

Strip the diodes away and this is all that is left — a double-pole switch that the carrier throws back and forth:

Carrier positive half-cycle

One opposite pair of diodes is forward-biased and conducts; the other pair is reverse-biased and open.

The message reaches the output transformer the right way up.

vout = + x(t)

Carrier negative half-cycle

The carrier reverses, so the other opposite pair conducts and the first pair goes open.

That pair connects the message transformer to the output transformer with the two wires swapped.

vout = − x(t)

message x(t) carrier / conducting path output diode off (open circuit)

Diode labels are not standard. Your notes say "D1, D2 on" for the positive half; other books say "D1, D3". What matters is that the conducting pair is always an opposite pair across the ring — two diodes that between them form a straight-through path, or the swapped path. Don't memorise numbers; memorise opposite pair conducts, the other pair is open.

3. The three waveforms

Same time axis for all three. The output is literally the top trace multiplied by the middle trace.

x(t)  message c(t)  carrier switching function (±1) vout(t) = x(t)·c(t)

Notice the dashed blue outline on the bottom trace: the output keeps touching +x(t) and −x(t) and never goes outside them. The message is the envelope — but a signed one, which is exactly why a simple envelope detector cannot demodulate this.

4. Why the diodes switch on the carrier and not on the message

This is the line you underlined in your notes, and it is the one assumption the whole analysis rests on.

A diode conducts when it is forward-biased. In this circuit two voltages are competing to bias it: the carrier, amplitude Ac, and the message, amplitude |x(t)|. We deliberately make the carrier much bigger:

Ac  ≫  |x(t)|

What this buys us. If the carrier dominates, the on/off pattern of the diodes depends only on the sign of the carrier — it is a clean, message-independent square wave at fc. That is what lets us write v_out = x(t)·c(t) with c(t) a fixed ±1 square wave.

What happens if you break it. If |x(t)| ever approached Ac, the message could flip a diode on its own. The switching would become message-dependent, c(t) would no longer be a fixed square wave, and the output would pick up nonlinear distortion products. The multiplication would stop being a clean multiplication.

So the ring modulator is a nonlinear circuit deliberately operated so that it behaves linearly with respect to the message. It is linear in x(t) and grossly nonlinear in the carrier — and that asymmetry is the design.

5. From a square wave to a sine: the Fourier step

We have v_out = x(t)·c(t), but DSB-SC is supposed to be x(t)·cos(2πf_c t) — a sinusoid, not a square wave. The Fourier series is what closes the gap.

c(t) = (4/π) cos(2πfct) − (4/3π) cos(6πfct) + (4/5π) cos(10πfct) − …

Drag the slider to add harmonics and watch the sum become the square wave. The point is the reverse direction: the square wave already contains a pure cosine at fc, plus unwanted stuff at 3fc, 5fc, …

With one term you have a plain cosine at fc. Every extra odd harmonic sharpens the corners. The ripple near the jumps never disappears — that is the Gibbs phenomenon, and it is harmless here.

Two properties of c(t) do all the work:

  1. No DC term. c(t) spends equal time at +1 and −1, so its average is zero. This is why the carrier is suppressed.
  2. Only odd harmonics, at 3fc, 5fc, 7fc, … — far away from fc, so a filter can throw them out.

6. What comes out, in the frequency domain

Multiply x(t) by each harmonic in turn and apply the modulation property — each cosine puts a scaled copy of the message spectrum around its own frequency:

vout(t) = (4/π) x(t)cos(2πfct) − (4/3π) x(t)cos(6πfct) + …

So the spectrum is copies of the message centred at fc, 3fc, 5fc, … with shrinking amplitudes. The bandpass filter keeps the first one and discards the rest.

Showing the raw ring output — copies at every odd harmonic.

Only positive frequencies drawn. Each triangle is a copy of the message spectrum, width 2W. Hover a copy for its amplitude.

The filter has to fit between the copies. The wanted band runs from fc−W to fc+W; the nearest unwanted band starts at 3fc−W. Requiring no overlap:

fc + W < 3fc − W   ⟹   fc > W

Always true in practice (carriers are megahertz, messages kilohertz), but it is a one-mark statement if a question asks for the condition.

7. The final result — and the factor everyone drops

vBPF(t) = (4/π) · x(t) · cos(2πfct)

This is DSB-SC. The gain is 4/π ≈ 1.27, not 1.

That 4/π is the amplitude of the fundamental in the Fourier series. It gets dropped constantly, and it matters the moment a question asks for transmitted power — power goes as amplitude squared, so dropping it costs you a factor of (4/π)² ≈ 1.62.

8. Why it's suppressed-carrier — the one-line reason

Compare two switching schemes that look nearly identical:

CircuitWhat gets switchedSwitching functionOutput after BPF at fc
Ring modulator the message alone, x(t) bipolar c(t) = ±1
(4/π)cos(2πfct) − (4/3π)cos(6πfct) + …
no DC term		 (4/π) x(t)cos(2πfct)
DSB-SC — no carrier line
Single-diode switching modulator message + carrier, x(t) + A cos(2πfct) unipolar s(t) = 0 or 1
½ + (2/π)cos(2πfct) − (2/3π)cos(6πfct) + …
DC term of ½		 (A/2)cos(2πfct) + (2/π)x(t)cos(2πfct)
DSB-AM — carrier present

Work the second row through and you'll see where the carrier line comes from. The diode passes [x(t) + A cos(2πf_c t)] · s(t), and s(t) contains a constant ½. That ½ multiplies the carrier term and hands you (A/2)cos(2πf_c t) straight at the output — an undiminished spectral line at fc. Meanwhile the ½ multiplying x(t) gives a baseband term the filter throws away.

The ring modulator never gets the chance to do this, for two reasons: only x(t) enters the ring, and its switching function has no DC component. Note that s(t) = (1 + c(t))/2 — the two switching functions differ by exactly a DC offset, and that offset is the whole difference between suppressed-carrier and full-carrier AM.

The word for this is "balance". The ring modulator is double-balanced: neither the carrier nor the message appears at the output on its own — only their product does. The single-diode circuit is single-balanced.

The center-tapped transformers are what enforce it. The carrier is injected at the center tap of T2's primary, so it drives both halves of that winding equally and in opposition — the two halves induce equal and opposite voltages in the secondary, and the carrier cancels. The message, arriving across the ends of the winding, does not cancel. That geometric symmetry is why the carrier is suppressed in hardware, not just on paper.

9. Checklist for the quiz

  • Function: product modulator, output = x(t)·c(t), yields DSB-SC after bandpass filtering.
  • Mechanism: the carrier square wave switches an opposite pair of diodes on each half-cycle; one pair passes the message straight through, the other passes it with the leads swapped, i.e. inverted.
  • Required condition: Ac ≫ |x(t)| so the switching is set by the carrier alone.
  • Filter condition: fc > W so the copy at fc doesn't collide with the one at 3fc.
  • Output: (4/π) x(t)cos(2πfct) — keep the 4/π.
  • Why SC: c(t) has zero DC component. Double-balanced.
  • Demodulation: coherent detection only — multiply by cos(2πfct + φ) and lowpass; output scales as cos φ, so it vanishes at φ = 90°. Envelope detection fails because x(t) changes sign.
  • Advantages: no external power needed (passive), good carrier suppression from the symmetry, works over a wide frequency range.
